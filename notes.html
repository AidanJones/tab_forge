The patterns behind the names

Natural notes are A, B, C, D, E, F and G. Each fret is one semitone. There is one fret between B–C and E–F; the other neighbouring natural notes are two frets apart.

C2 fretsD2E1F2G2A2B1C

The 12th fret repeats the open-string name one octave higher. Both E strings have the same note names at the same frets, two octaves apart.

A small first practice

  1. Explore low E. Say each natural note aloud as you play it.
  2. Remember G–A–B at frets 3–5–7, then C–D–E at 8–10–12. F is at fret 1.
  3. Switch to Find a note, using frets 0–5 first. Add the A string, then extend to fret 12.
  4. Try Name a note, then move on to the other strings.
What do the mnemonics mean?
Father Christmas Gave Dad An Electric Bass
F C G D A E B: the order that sharps are added in key signatures. “Blanket” is a common version of the final word. One sharp is F♯; two are F♯ and C♯. This also follows fourths when read backwards.
BEAD GCF
B E A D G C F: the order that flats are added. One flat is B♭; two are B♭ and E♭. Read as natural notes, this sequence moves up in perfect fourths, which helps with string-to-string patterns. It is not the guitar’s open-string order: the G–B strings are a major third apart.
G–A–B
Three natural notes you can use as landmarks: on either E string they sit at frets 3, 5 and 7. The phrase alone is not a universal mnemonic; its meaning depends on the lesson where you heard it.
Open strings, thickest to thinnest
E A D G B E. For example, “Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie.” Guitar string numbers run the other way: high e is string 1, low E is string 6.
